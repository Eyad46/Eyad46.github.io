Aerospace hardware can't fly until someone writes down every way it can fail.

That document is an FMEA. Under human-rated standards, every failure mode on it gets life-cycled on a test stand — so the list isn't paperwork, it's the test campaign. Today it's written by hand in spreadsheets. Faultline drafts it. The engineer signs it.

The wedge is teams that are both unblocked and gated — running real propellant through formal review gates, with no export-control regime preventing me from serving them. All ten I've mapped are university programmes. They have real hardware and near-zero budget: they're the right first users because they'll tell me when the output is wrong, not because they'll pay. Revenue comes later, from a different cohort.

02FaultlineThe problem

“Figuring out what could break is actually the easy part. The real, painful work is almost always the paperwork, the traceability, and getting everyone to agree on it.”

Safety & Mission AssuranceOrion program · NASA · 15 years
Why it costs money, not just hours
  • Miss a mode and that failure path is never tested. It flies untested.
  • Invent a junk one and you burn real time on a real test stand.
  • Weeks of senior engineering time per subsystem — and it's the work everyone defers until a review forces it.
  • The tooling that exists stores an FMEA you already wrote. Nothing helps you produce the reasoning, which is the expensive part.

“If you can't defend the paper trail with rock-solid evidence, the analysis basically doesn't exist.” — same engineer

03FaultlineWhat it is
AI proposes · engineer disposes

The control structure is the product.

  • Describe a subsystem. It drafts the failure modes, each with the reasoning written out.
  • Every severity, occurrence and detection score is anchored to a published rubric with a written justification.
  • The engineer accepts, rewrites or deletes every line, and signs it.
  • The export keeps a full diff of what the model proposed against what the engineer changed.
  • The occurrence floor is enforced in code. The model can't be optimistic about failure probability. Only the human can.

Output: 28-column CSV and a report-grade PDF built for design reviews.

07FaultlineThe honest gap
Say it before you're asked

No users. Nobody has run it on their own hardware.

Why
  • The engineers who feel this most are at SpaceX, Blue Origin and NASA. ITAR means they legally cannot put controlled hardware data into an outside tool built by a foreign person.
  • So I've used them differently: they tell me where the analysis is wrong. That's approach validation, not demand proof — and I'd rather name the difference than blur it.
What closes it
  • Unblocked AND gated. Not every reachable team needs this — most don't, and slide 08 says so. The ten that do run real propellant through formal review gates and have already walked me through their process. One has agreed to review output on a fluids component she owns — that thread is live now. Target: three teams running it on their own hardware, and one document defended at a real review gate, by end of Q4 2026.
  • Classification, not company name, decides who I can serve. Components under EAR 9x515 or EAR99 are servable today, and the deemed-export route there reaches non-employees. Components on the USML need an authorization the customer has to sponsor — which is a commercial constraint, not a legal impossibility.
  • Architecture handles the data question. Deployed inside the customer's own environment, the customer is the exporter of its own data and I'm not in the chain. Both agencies have said so. GroundControl, a YC company selling to this same buyer, took the FedRAMP version of that road.
  • What architecture doesn't fix is who's in the room. Prime supplier certifications require a US-person representative on customer-facing technical work. That's the real gate, and a US-person cofounder is necessary for it — but not sufficient on its own, and I'd rather say that than pretend one hire clears it.
08FaultlineWho it's for

The boundary isn't company size. It's whether one person can still hold the whole system in their head.

Above the line — the buyer
  • Reliability, mission assurance, product assurance and quality engineers who author and defend these documents.
  • Demand lives wherever an outside body, a customer, or a standard makes formal failure analysis mandatory — human-rating requirements, AS9100, customer qual gates.
  • Teams running real propellant and real test stands, who already keep a structured risk list and use it to decide what gets tested.
Below it — not the buyer, and I'll say so
  • “We were well aware of all the risks. All the teams are always in close contact… filling in the matrix just felt like a formality.”
  • “Forcing it on us right now would just be theater.”
  • Six teams told me plainly they don't run formal FMEAs. Knowing who doesn't need this is how I know who does.
09FaultlineCompetition & market
What I'm really competing with
  • Excel plus a senior engineer's memory. That's what almost every team actually uses. A vehicle systems safety engineer at Blue Origin put it plainly: “in the industry today, there is no one way that is known as the correct way to do it. A lot of what is being used is trial and error.”
  • Relyence, Isograph, APIS IQ-FMEA — real tools, but databases. Places to store an FMEA you already wrote.
  • Ansys medini, Siemens Polarion, PLATO — serious safety-analysis suites, but built around automotive and IEC/ISO functional-safety workflows and sold top-down into large organisations.
  • SpaceGrade — AI across the aerospace stack, FMEA as one item in a long feature list. They can add depth faster than I can add distribution — which is why the wedge is depth in one workflow, not breadth. A platform that treats FMEA as a checkbox produces output nobody signs, and the thing that makes it signable — anchored rubrics, an enforced floor, a proposed-versus-signed diff, a trace a review board accepts — is the whole product, not a feature.
  • The real threat: a customer's own team wiring an LLM to their Excel template over a weekend. That produces modes. It doesn't produce anchored scoring, an enforced occurrence floor, a diff of what the model proposed versus what the engineer changed, or a trace that survives a review board. That gap is the product.
  • GroundControl (YC) — AS9102 first-article inspection. Same buyer, opposite end of the lifecycle: proving a part was built to spec after it exists. I'm upstream, on the reasoning about what could fail.
Money
  • Priced per program or per review gate, not per seat — reliability is a thin slice of headcount, but the budget sits with the program that can't fly without the document. Published reliability tools run roughly $5–10k per seat per year as a floor reference.
  • ~10,000 AS9100-certified US sites plus ~9,000 US aerospace and defence establishments, each running multiple gated programs per year. Every human-rated or customer-qualified program is a recurring obligation, not a one-time seat.
  • Aerospace is the hardest version of a problem legally mandated in much bigger industries: AIAG-VDA across automotive, ISO 14971 for medical devices, plus energy and rail. Strictest first is the easy direction of travel.
  • What compounds, precisely. Not cross-customer data — controlled data never leaves the boundary, so I won't claim a network effect I can't have. What compounds is the rubric and failure-mode taxonomy built from public standards and published mishap reports, plus per-customer accumulation inside their own instance: every accept, edit and reject makes their analysis library better for them. That's a switching cost, and it's the honest version.
10FaultlineWhere I am · what I need

Built solo in eight weeks. I'm seventeen, still in high school, and not going to university.

Before this I spent a year taking NOVA-1, a two-stage fully reusable heavy-lift launch vehicle, from a blank page through a complete preliminary design review — about twenty documents across propulsion, structures, trajectory, GNC, avionics, recovery, thermal protection and cost — then ran an adversarial audit against my own work to find where I'd fudged the numbers. The failure analysis was the part that nearly stopped me. Faultline came out of that.

Who carries the liability
If a drafted mode is missed and hardware is lost, the engineer who signed it carries it — same as today. Nothing exports unsigned. Every score is anchored to a published rubric, the occurrence floor is enforced in code so the model can't be optimistic, and the export keeps a diff of what was proposed against what was changed. The audit trail is stronger than a spreadsheet's, not weaker.
Cofounder — status, not aspiration
A reliability or mission assurance engineer who has authored and defended FMEAs in a real program. 30% reserved. Honest status: no offer made and no candidate in process. The 56 conversations have doubled as the filter, and three people have engaged deeply enough to be worth a real approach once there's a user.
What I'm asking for
Not money yet — no entity, so I can't take it. I'm asking for intros to teams that own unclassified hardware and are gated by a customer or standard. Three teams running it on their own hardware, and one document defended at a review gate. That's the milestone that changes this from a good analysis into a company, and it's what I'd raise against.

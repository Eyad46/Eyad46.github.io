01 / IDENTITY

Seventeen years old. Cairo. No university, no team, no funding — yet.

I taught myself the engineering discipline of launch vehicle design over the last year and produced a complete preliminary design review package — twenty interlinked documents covering propulsion, structures, guidance, avionics, manufacturing, cost, and test plans.

Then I commissioned an adversarial review against my own work — the kind a diligence engineer at a venture fund would run. Five showstoppers and five serious issues were surfaced. Two of the five were closed analytically. One was defanged by engineering a bell-nozzle fallback that preserves most of the competitive edge. The remaining two are still in work, and confirming the aerospike efficiency the fallback hedges requires hardware testing — fundamentally not a paper problem anymore.

Then I built a sixteen-tool Python engineering toolkit that independently reproduces every number in the package — bottom-up vehicle mass, staging convergence, structural FEA, cooling analysis, POGO stability, cost models. Fifteen of fifteen verification checks pass. The toolkit also surfaced findings the original PDR missed: a HELIOS chamber wall over-temperature, an ENG-S1 throat-geometry inconsistency, a cost model 50–100% below comparable programs. All flagged honestly. None hidden.

What follows is the architecture, the numbers, and the honest list of what's still open. The case for it makes itself.

05 / ARCHITECTURE

Three decisions that define the cost model.

▸ DEC.01

Annular aerospike Stage 1, 44 engines, ORSC LOX/LCH₄.

Altitude-compensating nozzle eliminates the sea-level-vs-vacuum tradeoff inherent to bell nozzles. Differential throttling across four engine quadrants provides thrust vector control without gimbal hardware. The geometry only becomes stable at this vehicle's specific 3.9:1 aspect ratio — which is what makes pitched reentry possible.

375 sALT-COMPENSATED ISP · SL TO 80 KM
▸ DEC.02

Pitched-body Stage 1 reentry at 30–45° from vertical.

Falcon 9 returns engines-first because its aspect ratio forces it to. NOVA-1's aspect ratio permits a pitched return — full cylindrical face into the airflow, ~400 m² drag area, faster deceleration, lower peak heating. First-order analysis: positive static margin, grid fin authority 6.5× required restoring moment.

3.9 : 1ASPECT RATIO · STABILITY THRESHOLD
▸ DEC.03

Belly-first reusable Stage 2, designed in from day one.

Four deployable fins. Belly-into-velocity-vector reentry from orbit. Propulsive flip at 5 km altitude. Tower catch on forward hardpoints. Reuse cannot be retrofitted to a Stage 2 designed expendable — the structural margins, the TPS coverage, and the fin attachments all live in the original architecture.

06 / FALLBACK

The aerospike is upside. Not dependency.

The hardest question for any reviewer of NOVA-1 is the same one I asked myself: what if the aerospike doesn't perform? The 94% efficiency assumption traces to data from a cancelled NASA program with different propellants, chamber pressure, and geometry. It's the largest single uncertainty in the package — and it cannot be resolved without hardware testing.

So I engineered the answer: a bell-nozzle fallback configuration, fully designed to the same depth as the primary architecture. Same ORSC cycle, same propellants, same chamber pressure, same upstream propulsion. Only the nozzle section changes. The bell delivers Raptor v3-class performance using flight-proven cooling technology.

The competitive edge does not depend on the aerospike. It depends on vehicle scale, methane propellant economics, and the catch-recovery architecture — three things the fallback preserves entirely. The aerospike is the upside if DE-03 confirms efficiency. The bell case is what makes NOVA-1 investable regardless.
